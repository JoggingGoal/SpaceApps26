WHAT CAN GO WRONG?

01

Erosion

Wind and flowing water remove topsoil, sometimes taking organic matter and nutrients with it. Exposed ground is especially vulnerable.

02

Compaction

Heavy traffic and unsuitable field conditions compress pores, restricting roots and reducing infiltration.

03

Loss of organic matter

Repeated disturbance, low residue return and limited living roots can reduce the organic inputs that sustain soil structure and biological activity.

04

Nutrient imbalance and salinity

Nutrient depletion, excess inputs, and salt accumulation can restrict crop growth. The right response requires soil tests and local information.

DEEP DIVE 01How does soil degradation affect farms and climate?+

Why farmers care

Less effective soil structure can mean greater runoff, more difficult root growth and more variable crop performance. Lost topsoil can be costly or difficult to replace on a farming timescale.

Why climate matters

Soil contains substantial organic carbon. Soil management can influence carbon inputs, decomposition and losses, but any claim of net carbon storage requires measurements and an appropriate time horizon.

GLOBAL CONTEXT~33%

A 2024 peer-reviewed global review reported approximately one-third of soils as moderately to highly degraded across multiple degradation indicators. This is a broad global assessment, not a measurement of any particular farm.

CHAPTER 02 / UNDER THE SURFACE

What makes soil healthy?

Soil health combines physical, chemical, and biological properties. No single satellite value can measure them all.

◎PHYSICAL

Structure & water

Soil aggregates, pore space, rooting depth, compaction and infiltration determine how water and air move.

Ask: Does water enter the soil or run off?
♧BIOLOGICAL

Life & nutrient cycling

Roots, fungi, bacteria, earthworms and other organisms help decompose residues and recycle nutrients.

Ask: Is there food and habitat for soil life?
◇CHEMICAL

Nutrition & balance

pH, nutrients, salinity and organic carbon influence plant access to nutrients and overall soil function.

Ask: What do local soil tests reveal?

DEEP DIVE 03What is a “good” value for rain, heat or moisture?+

There is no single ideal rainfall or temperature for every farm. What matters is the crop, its development stage, soil water capacity, drainage, irrigation, and the timing of the weather. Comparing a location against its own historical seasons is a useful starting point.

Less rainfall ≠ always crop failure

A crop may still have access to stored soil water or irrigation, depending on local conditions.

More rainfall ≠ always better

Excess water can cause waterlogging, runoff and nutrient losses, particularly on vulnerable fields.

Our “hot day” cutoff of 32°C and “dry day” cutoff of 1 mm are transparent screening definitions, not universal biological thresholds.

DATA NOTESWhat are these charts actually measuring?+

Data are NASA POWER daily point values for the coordinates selected in Explore the Land. We calculate April–September rainfall sums, counts of daily maximum temperature ≥ 32°C, and the longest sequence of days receiving less than 1 mm of precipitation. April–September is a demonstration growing-season window and is not universally correct for all countries or crops.

Because observations are regional and can mix observations and reanalysis products, they should be checked against local stations and farm measurements before making decisions. Data are not equivalent to a satellite measurement of soil organic matter, yield, or profit.

CHAPTER 04 / WORKING WITH NATURE

What can farmers actually change?

The best soil-health strategy is usually a combination of practices adapted to local agronomy, equipment, climate and economics.

01 / PROTECT

Keep soil covered

Residue, mulch and cover crops can shield the surface and reduce erosion risk.

Tradeoff: establishment, termination and potential water competition.
02 / DISTURB LESS

Reduce unnecessary tillage

Less disturbance helps preserve structure and can reduce fuel and labor use.

Tradeoff: soil warming, weed control and equipment adjustments.
03 / DIVERSIFY

Rotate crop families

Diverse crops and roots can support nutrient cycling and interrupt some pest cycles.

Tradeoff: crop markets, machinery and timing requirements.
04 / MEASURE

Test soil & manage nutrients

Use soil testing and farm records to target amendments and avoid overapplication.

Tradeoff: testing costs, interpretation and local variability.

DEEP DIVE 04What are the limitations of cover crops and no-till?+

Cover crops may use soil moisture while growing. Timing of termination matters before cash crops. Reduced tillage may change weed management, surface residue conditions and planting equipment. Some soils require drainage or compaction remediation. No single practice is best everywhere.

That is why The Green Revolution's simulator compares strategies and tradeoffs rather than promising higher yields from every regenerative practice.

CHAPTER 05 / RESILIENCE & LIVELIHOODS

Healthy soil should work for farmers, too.

Soil stewardship can influence spending, productivity, risk and long-term land value. But profitability depends on local prices, yield response, practice costs and time.

Where benefits may come from

01
Input efficiency

Better nutrient and water management may reduce wasted fertilizer, fuel or irrigation.

02
Risk reduction

Infiltration and soil cover may help buffer weather variability, though benefits differ across fields.

03
Long-term productivity

Maintaining soil structure and fertility helps preserve productive capacity over time.

DEEP DIVE 05Can improving soil health help address climate change?+

Land and agricultural management influence greenhouse-gas emissions, soil carbon and resilience to heat and precipitation extremes. Practices such as reduced disturbance, cover crops and diverse rotations can be useful, but impacts depend on soil, weather, nitrogen management and time. Increased soil organic carbon does not automatically mean a whole farm is climate-neutral.

FieldShift does not estimate carbon credits, verified sequestration or future profits. Those claims would require monitoring, accounting boundaries and specialized models.

Evidence first. Decisions second.

Our mission includes education: users should always know what is an observation, a scientific relationship, a model estimate or an unmeasured unknown.

Heat-day counts, rainfall averages, dry-spell duration and growing degree days calculated from the weather record.

Farm-specific inputs

Crop calendars, cover estimates and water-balance calculations help compare strategies but require validation.
